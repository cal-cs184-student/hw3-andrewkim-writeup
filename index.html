Overview

In this assignment, I implemented a physically-based path tracer that simulates the physics of light in order to create highly realistic images. We started by creating the means to do this through ray intersection by shooting a ray from our camera, through a screen, and into the scene we want to render an image of. Naturally, all of the processes in this assignment required a lot of time to calculate, so we then implemented a way to speed them up through Bounding Volume Hierarchy, similar to bounding boxes we used in previous assignments. Finally, we implemented the meat of the assignment, the behavior of light itself, for both direct and global illumination, ending by implementing an even faster way to render images, adaptive sampling.

Something interesting I really enjoyed in this assignment was the process of converting real life physics and light into a computer to simulate. We didn’t have to specify exactly where a shadow falls or what color gets reflected onto nearby objects to the computer. Part 1: Ray Generation and Scene Intersection

The ray generation part of the rendering pipeline enables us to take a 3-dimensional scene and convert it into a 2-dimensional image on our screen. To understand the process, it is important to understand the numerous coordinate spaces we work in when doing so, namely the world space and camera space. The world space is the environment of the 3D scene where objects are placed at locations with coordinates that never change. The camera space is from the perspective of the viewer, like first-person mode in a video game, as they look around the scene. These coordinates stay unchanged relative to the viewer, but the camera’s location in the scene does change as it moves around. To create 2D images of a 3D scene, we use the help of a “virtual window” that we can visualize as a screen set up right in front of the camera. This window acts as the 2D display that is your screen, and it will help us determine what color each pixel should be as we look through the window into the scene.

In ray generation, we shoot rays from the camera through a pixel (on the virtual window) and into the scene until they hit some object. To start, we take the normalized coordinates of that pixel and transform them into camera space using our field of view angles (fov), which tell us the area of the scene our camera can see. Using the fact that the camera looks down the -Z axis, where the window sits 1 unit away, we calibrate the image coordinates with the 3D camera space coordinates. Our camera sits at (0, 0, 0) in camera space, which is where our ray will start as we then “shoot” it through these calibrated coordinates, normalizing the resulting vector. Finally, we take everything we have and move it into world space. Since our camera moves around, it isn’t always at (0, 0, 0) in world space, so we have to adjust so that our rays are correctly angled to hit the right objects. To do this, transform the ray’s origin and direction vector into world space using the c2w matrix, which positions the ray based on where the camera is pointing. We also adjust the camera’s view of anything that is too close or too far by setting min_t and max_t to nclip and fclip, respectively. We end by creating a Ray object to package everything together.

After shooting a ray into the scene, we run intersection tests to see what it actually hits. There could be multiple objects in its path, but we only care about the closest one since that is what the camera will see. For every object it hits, we check if it is closer than our current closest hit, and if so, we record relevant information about the collision and update max_t. The triangle intersection algorithm I implemented gives us this information about the collision. I integrated the Möller-Trumbore algorithm to optimize the process of finding the triangle that the ray hits, which uses dot and cross products to solve a system of linear equations based on the edge vectors of the triangle, giving us t and the Barycentric coordinates (u and v). We use these to verify that the ray does in fact hit inside the triangle, checking if t is within min_t and max_t and if u, v, and w are all between 0 and 1. We also use the Barycentric coordinates to linearly interpolate the three vertex normals and find the surface normal at the intersection point. With a valid intersection, we end by filling the *isect structure with the information about the collision: the t-value, the surface normal, the primitive that was intersected, and the surface material, all at that point.

Part 2: Bounding Volume Hierarchy

My BVH construction algorithm is implemented using the binary tree structure as explained in the project spec. At a high-level, we group primitives up into boxes that we then check whether rays hit, enabling us to eliminate entire boxes at a time if no collisions occur and thus speed up the rendering process.

To begin, we use recursion on the list of primitives we receive in the parameters as suggested in the spec. We create a bounding box bbox as well as initialize a counter variable numObjects to keep track of how many primitives are in the list. Running through the list using the iterators start and end, we expand our bounding box to see how “big” our group of primitives in the scene is and count them up using numObjects. Both of these will help us in the function later on. We end this first part of the function by storing our expanded bounding box as a new BVHNode.

The base case checks if the list of primitives we were given is small enough to make a leaf node out of them (no larger than max_leaf_size). If it is, the node we created is modified to set its start and end iterators to start and end, respectively, as well as its children l and r to NULL, returning the node to end the recursion.

If this node is not a leaf node, our recursive calls will be on both the left and right sides of the group after we split it up. We first find where to split the group of primitives by finding the longest axis (x, y, or z) and the splitPoint. The axis is found by comparing the dimensions of the bounding box’s extent. splitPoint is determined based on this axis and the average centroids along it, which is the heuristic I chose for picking the splitting point since it is based on where the geometry is clustered. We calculate this average by adding up the centroids and dividing by numObjects we found at the beginning of the function. Using these, we iterate through the list and collect all objects that are on the left side (< splitPoint) in a vector sortedObjects, counting the total number on this side along the way. We then collect all of the objects that are on the right side of the split point and add them to sortedObjects. At this stage, it is vital that we check whether or not all primitives ended up on one side to avoid infinite recursive calls. If they did, we empty out sortedObjects and add the primitives based on their order in the original list we were given, giving half of them to the left side. Regardless of whether we had to adjust for this case or not, we take our resulting sortedObjects vector and replace the original list with these elements. We now have all the components needed for the recursive call. We find the point where the left and right sides split, midPoint, by adding the number of objects on the left to start. This is what allows us to call the function on the left and right sides and set their return values to the left and right children respectively:

node->l = construct_bvh(start, midPoint, max_leaf_size);

node->r = construct_bvh(midPoint, end, max_leaf_size);

Once we have our children, we return the node to end the function.

Part 3: Direct Illumination

The direct lighting function can be used with either uniform hemisphere sampling or by importance sampling, both of which are used to simulate how light behaves in our 3D scene. These enable us to create realistic shadows and highlights when we have a light source that shines on the objects. In this part, we were concerned with rendering light bouncing one time before reaching our camera.

The direct lighting function using uniform hemisphere sampling was implemented following a two-step process. At a high-level, we first shoot a ray from the camera through a pixel and into the scene until we hit an object. Then, we use uniform hemisphere sampling and the Monte Carlo estimator to find out what color that location on the object is, which will be the color of our pixel.

To start the implementation, I modified estimate_direct_lighting_hemisphere() to run the Monte Carlo estimator by looping through num_sample directions to figure out the average lighting value at our location. At each pass, we sample a random direction using hemisphereSampler and calculate the probability density function (pdf) before converting this direction vector into world space coordinates by multiplying it by o2w. We then create a new Ray> object that will be shot into the scene in this randomly sampled direction, setting its min_t value to EPS_F in order to avoid numerical precision issues. The next step is to actually send the ray into the scene and check if it collides with any object using our previously implemented bvh->intersect() function. If we do collide, we store information about the collision, check if that object is a light source by getting its emission value, and use the material’s BSDF evaluation math from task 1 to calculate the amount of light that gets sent back to the camera. We divide each individual sample by its own pdf before adding it to a running sum. This sum is our total amount of accumulated light, which is then divided by num_samples at the end to get the final color for the pixel.

As for importance sampling, the process improves on the approach we used with uniform hemisphere sampling by looking at the light sources and sending rays directly to them instead of in random directions. This will improve how we look at and render shadows.

To implement importance sampling, I modified estimate_direct_lighting_importance() by looping through each light source in the scene. We first check what kind of light source the current one is to determine our sample rate: if it is a delta light source, we use one sample, otherwise we use ns_area_light, storing the value in num_samples. We then create a running sum of all the light from this source as well as a nested loop that will accumulate and add to this value. We loop through and sample num_samples times for the current light source, storing information about its direction, distance, and pdf as well as its radiance before converting the direction vector from world space to object space. At this point, as a quick safety measure we check if this light is behind the surface plane and skip this sample if it is. We continue on by creating a “shadow” ray to shoot from our surface to the light source (using EPS_F here as well to avoid numerical precision issues), fire it into the scene (only up to the distance of the light, minus EPS_F), and check if it intersects with anything. If it does, that means an object is blocking the light and we are in a shadow, which means we should skip this sample as well. Otherwise, we reach the light source and then use our BSDF evaluation math to find out how much light our surface material reflects, ending this iteration of the nested loop by adding the amount of light this ray is contributing to our surface point to our running sum. We end the outer loop by averaging the brightness of the current light source by dividing our sum by num_samples and adding it to L_out, the global running sum for the entire scene. The function itself ends by returning this final brightness and thus the pixel color.

To combine both approaches of the direct lighting function, I modified one_bounce_radiance() that calls either estimate_direct_lighting_hemisphere() or estimate_direct_lighting_importance() depending on which one the user wants to use given the direct_hemisphere_sample flag.

Part 4: Global Illumination

The indirect lighting function enables us to produce even more realistic images by not only calculating the amount of direct light an object reflects, but also the indirect light coming from objects around it. In reality, light bounces off everything and everywhere, where objects are lit up by a combination of all the light that reaches them. This is done by increasing the amount of bounces we allow light to have from just the zero or one bounce we used previously. The results are effects called color bleeding, where one object’s color influences the color of another object the light bounces off to, and ambient shadows, soft diffuse shadows that appear in tight corners and where objects meet a surface.

My implementation of the indirect lighting function builds off of what we did in part 3. We begin by using the sampler to pick a random direction from our collision point and shooting a single ray that way in search of light (if we sample multiple times per pixel, we will average out the colors returned by these rays). We then dive into the core of the entire process, the function at_least_one_bounce_radiance(), which calculates light at our surface point and then recursively calls itself to estimate and add up subsequent radiances until we reach our bounce limit.

This function starts by accumulating all direct light for this surface point into a running sum, L_out, if isAccumBounces is set to true by calling one_bounce_radiance(). If it is false, we add direct light to L_out only if this is the final bounce (i.e., r.depth == 1). We then check our base case: if we reach our depth limit of max_ray_depth, stop and return the light we have so far (when r.depth <= 1). Otherwise, we bounce to the next object. To prevent our rays from bouncing forever, I also implemented another termination approach known as Russian Roulette at this point in the function that will work together with the hard limit of max_ray_depth. This process uses a termination probability of 0.35 (0.65 survival probability) and then flips a coin based on this probability. If a ray survives, we’ll scale it accordingly so as to prevent bias and compensate for lost rays by boosting the brightness of those that survived. If our ray dies, we return L_out without adding indirect light. Note that Russian Roulette is not used on the first bounce to ensure each pixel gets at least one indirect bounce.

If we make it past the termination checks, we continue finding out where light bounced from. We pick a direction randomly to check if light is indeed coming from that way as well as get the BSDF of this intersection point to know how much of that incoming light will be reflected or absorbed. If our chosen direction is valid, we convert it from object to world space by multiplying it by o2w. We then create a new Ray> object starting from our collision point pointed in this direction and prepare it by setting min_t to EPS_F to avoid numerical precision issues and by reducing the ray’s depth by one from the previous ray. Once it’s ready, we fire it into the scene and check if it hits anything using our previously implemented bvh->intersect() function again. This function call serves as another termination check: if we don’t hit anything, return the light we have so far. Otherwise, we are all set up and ready to recursively perform this process again on the fired ray and the next object hit. The light returned from these deeper recursive calls represents the indirect light from the rest of the scene in that direction.

When we get back the indirect light, we have to calculate the angle it is arriving at. A light beam hitting a surface head on will have a stronger effect than a beam hitting it at a shallower angle (captured by Lambert’s cosine law), which is important when calculating how much light gets reflected. The cosine of this angle is simply the z-component of our sampled direction. The true magic then happens here when we apply the rendering equation: we take the light from our deeper recursive calls, multiply it by this object’s BSDF value, scale it by this cosine term, and divide it by the pdf to correctly weight the final contribution. Since we know at this point we survived Russian Roulette (if it is used), we compensate for the lost rays by boosting this ray’s color to prevent bias as mentioned above by dividing it by the survival probability (0.65).

The last part of our function determines how to set L_out based on the mode we are in. If we are rendering the image, isAccumBounces will be set to true, meaning we should add the light from every bounce to our sum of total accumulated light. Otherwise, we are just checking the indirect light itself for debugging purposes, so we should set L_out to the indirect light directly (no accumulation). The final step is to return the final color, which will either be sent back to the previous recursive call or be used to color the pixel itself.

One last modification was made to est_radiance_global_illumination(). I removed the call to one_bounce_radiance() we previously had and replaced it with our newly implemented at_least_one_bounce_radiance() function since it accounts for one bounce already (we call one_bounce_radiance() within it).

Part 5: Adaptive Sampling

Up to this point we have improved the quality of our images by simply shooting more rays through each pixel and waiting large amounts of time for our images to render. We shot the same amount of rays across each pixel regardless of how simple or detailed the objects actually were at those points. Not every pixel needs this much work. We can instead save time by putting more effort into the more difficult parts of the scene and do less work where it isn’t needed through a process called adaptive sampling. In adaptive sampling, we can check the progress of each pixel and move on when we see the color stabilize. This will allow us to use less computation on pixels that don’t need it and more for those that do because we don’t waste time and rays on backgrounds with consistent colors. We still keep the sample rate high for more complicated areas to reduce the noise and reach our final converged result.

To implement adaptive sampling, I modified the raytrace_pixel() function to incorporate this functionality. I first created running sums of each ray’s brightness and their brightness squared (the illuminance) in addition to how many rays we’ve shot so far (we might not shoot all ns_aa rays). I then modified the loop to include the benefit of adaptive sampling - whether or not a pixel has converged to its final color. This if statement skips the 0th ray since we haven’t shot anything yet and it checks if the current ray is a multiple of samplesPerBatch so that we can save time by checking in batches instead of each individual ray. If both cases pass, we then move on to actually checking for convergence using the formulas provided in the project spec. We calculate the average brightness of the rays shot so far as well as the variance and standard deviation. At this point we can check if I, our pixel’s convergence, is small enough based on maxTolerance and mu for this pixel to be considered “done” and move on if it is. We end by getting the average color by dividing by how many rays we actually shot (not ns_aa) and updating our buffers sampleBuffer and sampleCountBuffer to generate the sampling rate heatmap.
